What Happens to Look-Alike Domains Before They Reach You

A list of look-alike domains is where the work begins: hundreds or thousands of names that look like yours, most of them unregistered, some parked at a broker, a few belonging to companies that simply share a word with you. Since version 4, HirdmanUI carries Brand Protection as a module of the client portal. We run the whole cycle on our side, and your portal holds a short list of decisions, each with its grounds and its evidence.

Generating look-alike names is the easy part

Open-source permutation tools produce thousands of candidates for one name, and their own documentation warns about false positives and asks you to inspect every hit regardless of its score. One published analysis of brand-impersonation alerts estimated that 133 similar-domain alerts cost analysts about 44 working hours, while only four of the domains needed further investigation.

So the question worth asking is what happens to the names before they reach you. Here is what happens to them at Hirdman.

Step 1 Your names, under your control

The watch list starts with your domains and brand names. Your team asks for a name to be watched or taken off watch from the portal, and the portal shows what became of the request, so half a year later it is still clear who asked, when and why.

Step 2 Finding the candidates

Typosquatting is one of more than 35 techniques. We expand each name through spelling permutation families: one character dropped, added, doubled or swapped with a neighbour, a keyboard-neighbour replacement, a vowel swap, an inserted hyphen or dot, a bit flip, a plural, the same name in another zone, the name plus a dictionary word, and characters replaced by ones that look the same. We also look for your brand joined with another word and measure each candidate's edit distance from your name. Certificates are a source of their own: our own Certificate Transparency service streams every new certificate, so we catch one issued for your name the moment it appears, even if the site lives for a couple of days.

Step 3 Most candidates are unregistered, and we show how many

A generated name is a guess until a registry says otherwise. In one client's published cycle, we struck out 1,082 candidates. The portal shows that number with its composition, because a single figure looks the same for careful work and for lost data: we generated and checked 1,052 permutations and found them unregistered, and for 30 more the registry itself answered that the name is not registered.

You receive the count, the reason, and the names behind it if you want to look.

Step 4 Measuring every name that exists

We measure 76 signals for each name that exists. Among them: whether it accepts mail and what its SPF policy says, the page title and HTTP response, whether the page holds password fields or forms, the registrar and registration date, the hosting organization, the name servers, the technology stack, and whether the page is parked. We store the evidence behind each measurement as files: screenshots, page HTML, the site icon, certificates and RDAP registration records, each keyed by its SHA-256.

The portal shows a screenshot of a look-alike next to a screenshot of your own site at the same scale, so you judge "does it look like us" with both pages in view.

Step 5 Grouping by icon, so one operator gets one decision

A forger copies a site whole and almost always drags the icon along. We use that.

Exact match

Every site icon gets a fingerprint. In one client cycle it sat on 154 look-alikes and 26 of the client's own assets. The analyst can copy it and search other indexes with it by hand. The portal keeps it to itself, because a lookup would tell a search engine which icons we are hunting for you.

Shape match

A second, 64-bit shape fingerprint catches a recolored or re-saved copy. Measured on a live cycle: the client's own mark in a different shade sits at distance 10, foreign icons at 14 to 39, against a threshold of 12. The portal draws the distance on a scale from "the same drawing" to "nothing in common".

Decisions by drawing

An icon can be marked as your mark, somebody else's mark, a domain broker's shop window or a site-builder's default. The last two matter: thousands of unrelated sites share a broker's or a builder's placeholder, and an icon match on them says only that both sites use the same placeholder. Deciding by drawing costs less than deciding name by name: in one cycle, seven icon decisions closed 29 hosts of the client's own perimeter, one of them closing 20. A decision about a drawing leaves the owner of each name as it was: in the same cycle, one mixed icon group held 2 of the client's hosts and 17 foreign ones.

Grouping by owner

Inside a bin, names can be grouped by registrar, address or icon, so one operator's names are worked together: ten names on one address make one case. Grouping reorders the list and keeps every name in it; the default order stays by danger.

Step 6 Scoring, then a person

Each name is judged by rules, by a model and, where it is disputed, by an analyst. The models are local: they run on our own hardware, and every name, page and record of yours is processed there. Every judgment records who produced it. At ingest, a machine judgment must name its model and prompt version, and a strong verdict must rest on a page someone looked at; we refuse anything else. When an analyst overrides the pipeline, the original judgment stays visible beside the new one, so an auditor sees what exactly was overridden.

Step 7 A quality gate before you see anything

A cycle reaches your portal only after it passes 51 named validation rules, from file structure to meaning. Two of them guard against the quietest failure in this field, a source that answers without an error and without data: every source has a liveness probe, an object it is known to hold and must find on each run, and we flag a source whose yield collapses against the previous cycle. So "No impersonators found" and "we can no longer search" always look different.

What lands in your portal

  • Each name carries a verdict, "Action required", "Monitor" or "Unrelated", with its grounds and an urgency in words: Highest, High, Medium or Low.
  • Each card says whether the name resolves, dated by the cycle that checked it: "Resolving · checked 23 Oct 2026", "Not resolving" or "Not measured".
  • Above the register, a coverage line says how many records each measurement covered and why the rest was not measured, because an empty column reads as "checked, clean".
  • The Brand page opens on what appeared since the last cycle. We declare a name gone only after it has been missing for several cycles; a single quiet run leaves it in place.
  • We flag takeover risk: a name of yours that still points at a released cloud resource (dangling DNS). Whoever claims that resource can publish under your name.
  • Proofs captured by the pipeline and proofs filed by hand sit in one evidence list, newest capture first, and the register filters by "With a capture", "Link only" or "Nothing attached".

From a mention to a takedown

A confirmed abuse becomes a case: a mobile app, a social account, a website, a messenger channel, a marketplace listing or an ad. A mobile app we take apart down to the code, to see what was changed and where the data goes; we have already taken several mobile app cases all the way to takedown. The case carries its proofs and downloads as a ZIP your lawyers can verify: readable file names, a README and a SHA-256 manifest, current files only or with history, up to 50 cases at once.

We draft takedown letters from what was measured, with visible brackets wherever a fact is missing, so each letter carries only the details of its own case. We record each letter and its outcome: awaiting an answer, taken down, declined or no answer, with a follow-up due after 48 hours. A batch of registrar reports carries only records with confirmed evidence, and we list every record left out with its reason, so you know what to collect next.

The Brand report comes in three editions: full, a two-page brief for management and a regulator edition. The full and regulator editions end with an attestation sheet to sign that repeats what was not checked.
