Methodology and red flags

Are you paying for analysis, or for what sits on torrents for free?

Questions for your breach monitoring and brand protection vendor, and how to tell a demonstrated answer from a written claim.

How confident are you in the quality of your vendor's breach monitoring, domain monitoring and brand protection? And is there anyone in your company who can judge it?

The market problem: for many vendors, "breach monitoring" means an export from public logs, the kind of torrent archive such as AlienTXT-Base-562GB with freely available leaks that you could download yourself at no cost. It is like paying a consultant for a report and getting a link to an encyclopedia. The real service is analysis, risk assessment, prioritization and saved time, not "here is your data".

a public torrent archive of leaks (AlienTXT-Base-562GB), free to download. If your vendor's "report" is a digest of such data, you are paying for what is already in the open.

Sound familiar? The vendor sends a CSV full of repeated junk with "fresh" dates, as if the breach happened days ago. Your team and the SOC spend days on it, and in the end the "breach" turns out to be several years old, or never existed at all. Asked directly, the vendor dodges: "well, we picked it up somewhere in the combo logs". Every finding becomes a new support ticket and days of waiting. The time gap works against you: what you untangle for weeks, an attacker uses within hours. While you dig through someone else's junk, the real breach is lost in the noise, and attackers see it too. Sometimes one missed breach is enough to put the business at risk. And that is only one red flag of many.

To make this easier to check, even without a specialist on staff, we collected questions and criteria for your vendor. The key point: do not read the answer (anyone can write anything), ask the vendor to show live how exactly they solve your task. Below, for each point, is how the task should be solved (this is how we work), so you have something to compare against, and which red flags to watch for at your vendor.

No pressure. If you already have a vendor, use these criteria to assess them; the document is useful on its own even if you never contact us. Clients often come to us to double-check the results of other contractors, which is why we show our methodology openly and stand behind the quality.

Data reliability and quality

01

Do you detect fake and generated breaches, and how exactly?

How it should be
  • Verification through password recovery or reset: does the account really exist.
  • Source and forum reputation: a known group with a history, or an actor with a negative rating who repackaged an old set as "new".
  • Pattern analysis of the database: recognizing generated passwords and synthetic records (identical salts, templated addresses, incremental IDs).
  • Cross-checking against already known dumps, so a repack is not taken for a new breach.
  • Quality of the primary source: a private forum and a first-hand dump versus a worked-over set mixed with fakes.
  • The actor's motivation and geopolitical context: during information conflicts the number of "breaches" invented for publicity grows.
Red flag: the vendor answers "we have filters" without describing the process; cannot tell a repackaged old set from a new breach; generated records reach the report as genuine.
02

How do you determine the freshness of a breach and the real date of compromise?

How it should be
  • Freshness is defined by the place in the data lifecycle, not by a date in the file. A breach has a path: intrusion, persistence in the infrastructure, dump (sometimes with a ransom), posting on a semi-closed forum for reputation and tokens, spread across public channels, repackaging with junk mixed in and release into public giveaways of ready login-password pairs ("free ULP").
  • Data is taken at the start of the chain, not at the end: direct monitoring of hundreds of hacker and ransomware groups in their channels gives access to the dump right after it appears in semi-private circles, before it is polluted and repackaged. Most services take from the end of the chain, already spoiled.
  • "Date loaded into the database" and "date of the actual intrusion" are strictly separated: as a rule only the attacker knows the real intrusion date, and until the database is worked through it cannot be established reliably, so every finding gets a confidence level instead of a false "freshness". Even the attacker does not always know it: those who plant a web shell sometimes find someone was already inside, and intruders can stay in a company for years unnoticed. A finding is therefore dated by when it appeared in the space we monitor. Freshness is confirmed by publication in the primary source and by the infrastructure inside the data (are the domains, panels and software versions live).
  • Infrastructure for the volume: originals often weigh terabytes and are scattered across thousands of links; they are collected by parallel streams and parsers, and the processing pipeline extracts only what is relevant (the useful part may sit in hundreds of thousands of PDFs or in correspondence with a client's key), discarding junk.
  • Hashed passwords are cracked on powerful computing resources.
  • The age of a breach is not its danger. Human weaknesses do not go away: in our cases a ten-year-old password regularly fits a client's current service, so an "old" breach is checked for relevance rather than discarded by date.
Red flag: data is taken at the end of the chain, from "free ULP" and public aggregators, already noisy and with planted "fresh" dates; the "date it appeared in the database" is presented as the "intrusion date"; no direct access to primary sources and no infrastructure to process a large dump, so deeply buried items (a key in a PDF, a secret in correspondence) are never found and hashes stay uncracked; a finding is written off as "old" without checking whether the password still works.
03

Do you work with infostealer logs, and if so, how?

How it should be
  • The basis is raw dumps: they contain much that ready login-password pairs and combo lists never have.
  • Sources are curated: it is known who constantly generates and repackages junk, and those are cut off; combo lists are checked with particular care.
  • Raw infostealer logs when needed, plus cooperation with specialized teams that focus on logs.
  • After removing a stealer, companies often overlook shadow IT and the employee's personal devices, which leads to repeat compromise; specific recommendations cover this.
Red flag: any combo lists are taken without checking the source, junk generators are not cut off; "infostealer logs" turn out to be the same login-password pairs without cookies, sessions or tokens; after a stealer incident the advice is a templated "change the password" that leaves shadow IT and personal devices open.
04

Is your "dark web" a set of public onion sites or real closed venues?

How it should be
  • Real access from inside the environment: entry to closed forums is earned through reputation in the underground and through expensive paid accounts.
  • In this field the "dark web" means closed venues, the priority source of primary data.
  • Often this means downloading tens of terabytes and extracting only what is needed; most services skip it because it costs too much. Extraction requires a complex ETL pipeline (Extract, Transform, Load) that parses heterogeneous data from thousands of files into a single usable format.
  • Preventive: on closed venues you can see an attack being prepared and planned, and access being sold, so there is time to warn the client in advance instead of cleaning up afterwards.
  • The best indicator of source quality is our own product: the same data is used in our own penetration tests, so the standard is "for ourselves".
Red flag: the "dark web" is a public onion site presented as a closed source; no real access and no resources to download and process large dumps; risks are discovered after the fact, reactively.
05

Which sources do you monitor, and can you name a specific list?

How it should be
  • Thousands of sources: public and private breach databases, dark web forums, data marketplaces, Telegram channels, ransomware group sites, infostealer logs. Our own database holds more than 106 billion records, plus another 46 billion raw records.
  • Group channels are covered from two sides: through aggregators and public ransomware and breach trackers, and directly from the forums where hackers publish their personal channels on social networks and messengers (X, Facebook, Telegram, Matrix), plus our own accumulated lists. Public trackers are only a base layer on top of closed sources that few have access to.
  • Early signals: news sites, leak aggregators and professional security and DLP channels, where the first mentions of a possible compromise appear earliest.
  • Targeted search on a trigger: when information about a potential breach appears or group activity spikes (including against the background of geopolitical events), the relevant databases are searched for deliberately across all available sources.
  • Some sources are closed: access to private venues where fresh databases and logs are posted is secured through our own methods of working with underground communities.
  • Partner sources in the underground deliver new breaches and logs among the first.
  • Every source goes through verification: export, validation of authenticity, cleaning from fakes, normalization, loading into the monitoring system through separate pipelines.
Red flag: a vague "many sources" without a specific list; collection is limited to public Telegram channels and aggregators; coverage is limited to breach databases; no early signals and no targeted search, they wait for the database to surface on its own.

Depth of analysis

06

Do you account for 2FA/MFA bypass risks based on breaches?

How it should be
  • It is taken into account that a stealer log may hold a live session (cookies, tokens) that bypasses MFA; when such a log is available, safe validation checks which services the session is still live for.
  • The strength of the 2FA/MFA itself is assessed by an established method: which factor type is used and whether it is vulnerable to session hijacking or a phishing kit.
  • Thanks to extensive penetration testing experience, 2FA/MFA bypass techniques are collected systematically: the AiTM class (Evilginx, Tycoon) and fresh vulnerabilities (0-day, 1-day).
  • Since most companies rely on MFA as their main defense, all of this goes into extended recommendations: revoking active sessions, rotating tokens and secrets, invalidating cookies and re-authenticating, on the assumption that the session may still be live, rather than a plain "change the password".
Red flag: advises only "change the password", ignoring that the stealer may hold a live session that bypasses MFA; does not assess the strength of the MFA itself and does not track bypass techniques (AiTM, Evilginx, fresh vulnerabilities).
07

Do you have a reverse check?

How it should be
  • A unique identifier from the breach is taken (login, the first part of an email, the password itself or a unique domain) and run through all sources again and again, in different combinations.
  • Every iteration opens new links: one more service, a personal account with a password that fits the corporate one, predictable password-change patterns; this is how the attacker's whole access chain is reconstructed.
  • The human factor: employees use one password at home and at work, so personal breaches are checked along with corporate ones; the link is visible through the email even with a different password (a personal account on a weak third-party site, a compromise, then work accounts).
  • Breaches with a bare "admin", "devsecops" or an ordinary Gmail address, with no corporate email at all, are caught: it is worked out that such a login belongs to a privileged account (administrator, moderator), not an ordinary user.
  • The method was built after many penetration tests: most attacks combine the human factor, password reuse and non-obvious links. Since the data has already leaked, the attacker may have used it, so the reverse check exposes these links and lowers the risk of repeat attacks by closing the routes the attacker could use to return.
Red flag: checks are superficial, "there is a login, a password, a URL, the password does not fit, risk closed"; personal and corporate accounts are not linked, the email link is missed when the password changes, privileges behind an anonymous login such as "admin" are not recognized.

08

What scope of data do you process in breaches?

How it should be
  • Not only login-password pairs: raw dumps, files, correspondence, financial documents, backups, configs.
  • Cookies and sessions, keys, tokens, certificates.
  • Full URLs with ports; the interface, the admin panel and the API are told apart.
  • The most critical risks are often next to the password rather than in it: internal correspondence, financial documents, customer personal data, backups, secrets and tokens; attacks are developed with them even without a password.
  • There is value even where there is no password at all: from a single phone number, social network profiles, correspondence and closed messenger groups are recovered; a dossier is assembled and the attack develops without a single password, up to targeted phishing.
  • One account from different breaches is stored separately, so the history of compromise and the links between incidents are visible.
Red flag: stores only the login, the password and the URL, aggressively "normalizing" and scrubbing the rest; delivers an anonymized list of "such-and-such email was found" with no context.
The most critical risks are often not in the password itself but in what sits next to it: correspondence, financial documents, customer personal data, backups, secrets and tokens.
09

Breaches as an investigation tool: do you take on such tasks?

How it should be
  • The entry point is determined (a service, a contractor, a device) and what else the attacker could have obtained from it is assessed.
  • Forensics: password analytics show who generated passwords, where and when, and how they changed them between services.
  • Insider search: the client provides logs and the logic, then forensics, OSINT on employees and breach analysis join in, and intersections in the leaked data are searched for.
  • This is especially relevant now: layoffs are widespread, and hackers increasingly buy data and access from disgruntled former employees; an internal offender is more dangerous than an external one because they know how things work inside.
  • This is not theory: such cases have already been closed successfully. At a customer's request, the internal offender behind a leak and resale of source code was identified through a combination of data patterns, the reverse check and analysis of employee correspondence.
  • Checking suspicious employees, what and where they write about the company, as part of brand protection.
Red flag: reports only the "fact of presence" without cause or scale; can only "show a list of breaches" and does not take non-standard tasks (forensics, insider search).
10

Depth of coverage: basic breaches only, or reconnaissance through the attacker's eyes?

How it should be
  • At the reconnaissance stage, the same picture the attacker sees is collected: subdomains and assets, open ports and services, resources behind VPN, vulnerable components and outdated versions (SCA), services without a password and insecure configurations.
  • A full inventory of digital assets: domains, subdomains, services, integrations, shadow IT. The real attack surface is visible, not only what is in plain sight.
  • Secrets are searched for unconventionally, the way an attacker searches: source code and JS bundles, forgotten configs and .env files, public repositories, reverse engineering of APKs (keys, tokens and endpoints inside a build are a leak in themselves).
  • Everything that grants access is found: API keys, CI/CD tokens, SSH keys, service accounts, tokens for AI tools; each key is checked for whether it is live or expired and what it opens.
  • Along the way: certificates, suspicious traffic (cloaking, redirects) and typosquatting domains.
  • This is a pre-pentest rather than a full penetration test, basic early checks with maximum return: on a domain without a single leaked email, 711 secrets were found in public build files, 120+ of them in one .env file (GitLab and CI tokens, deploy keys, AWS roles).
Red flag: looks only at the database of leaked passwords; secrets in code, configs and mobile builds, as well as the external surface (ports, shadow IT, vulnerable services, certificates), are out of scope; "no breaches on this domain" is read as "all clear".

Usefulness, noise and delivery

11

How do you remove noise and confirm findings?

How it should be
  • Only confirmed findings reach the portal; filtering out false positives is the vendor's job.
  • A word match is not yet a finding: when a brand name coincides with an ordinary word or is used by other companies, keyword search produces a stream of junk. Every match is checked for belonging to your company specifically, by domain, infrastructure, employees and the context of the record.
  • The share of confirmed findings can be shown.
Red flag: the client spends weeks tuning filters to sift out junk; brand search amounts to a keyword grep, and other companies and random matches end up in the report.
A false incident costs the team hours. A missed real one costs the business.
12

What does the client get: an export with counters, or a report built for their goals?

How it should be
  • For each finding: what happened, what the risk is, what to fix first and how the attacker will proceed.
  • Priority by a formula-based model (tiers): higher if the account is corporate (not personal), the record came from a stealer (not an old public dump), a weak password matched a stealer trace, plus the evidence base.
  • The report is generated automatically from the analytics and the same risk model: ranked risk with recommendations, not event counters.
  • At the Enterprise level, a detailed report built for the client's goals: identify an insider, find employees with poor digital hygiene or lower the overall risk; the work profile changes with the task.
  • At the customer's request, the report also covers employee risks.
  • The finished report is uploaded to the portal and available for download at any time; it reads well for both the business and the SOC analyst: a technical breakdown plus an executive summary.
Red flag: a single line "email found" without context or priority; an export with counters that you have to interpret yourself; an interface overloaded with modules (radars, attack maps, news parsing) that do nothing to lower the customer's risk; one report for everyone, with no adaptation to goals.
13

Do you take the work on yourself, or does the client sort it out alone?

How it should be
  • The client hands over only the domain; sources, reconnaissance, analysis and prioritization are run by the vendor; launch within 1 to 2 days, without weeks of onboarding.
  • Search, deduplication, filtering of false positives, risk assessment and mitigation are on the vendor's side, instead of "here is your export, sort it out".
  • The company is taken under continuous watch: new subdomains and assets, new open ports and services, infrastructure changes, and within the agreed scope brand protection too; control runs continuously without requests from the client.
  • Recurrence is tracked: has the same thing reappeared, or appeared on new infrastructure.
  • Alerts only for what matters: a confirmed significant event, not a stream of trivia.
  • Reconnaissance uses the same methods as the attacker; the difference is that you get the result, not them.
Red flag: the model is "here is your alert, the rest is on you"; a showcase portal that takes the client weeks to learn; infrastructure changes and new assets are not tracked, and the client learns about important things from the scheduled report.

Speed, integrations, price and trust

14

What is the delay between event and finding, and what does priority depend on?

How it should be
  • The delay is not always on the monitoring side: a hacker often posts a breach weeks or months after the intrusion. So the measure is the speed from the moment the data appears in the sources, not "time since the intrusion".
  • Once detected, the alert is immediate, through a dedicated agreed channel, without waiting for the scheduled report.
  • New domains and certificates are tracked in near real time (Certificate Transparency); the ETL pipeline runs around the clock: a match with a client alerts our team at once, and a confirmed risk goes to the client immediately.
  • On Enterprise, VIP accounts are monitored hourly.
  • Critical items, for example a secret in a public repository, are processed out of turn, with forced priority.
  • The frequency of some tasks depends on the plan: compliance checks, for example, may run on a weekly cycle.
Red flag: a single batch cycle "once a week or month" without prioritization; weeks of back-and-forth between the finding and the reaction.
15

Do you have an API and integrations, and in what form do you deliver data?

How it should be
  • An extensive API delivers portal data that complex algorithms have already processed, ready for a report; it can be pulled into practically any system of yours.
  • Metrics go to your dashboards: we deploy them turnkey and quickly.
  • The format is practically anything the client is interested in, including technical information and indicators of compromise; integrations with SIEM, SOAR and ticketing systems, with custom work done for the task.
  • There is an integration with our partner whitespots.io, a platform for Application Security, DevSecOps and ASPM (Application Security Posture Management).
Red flag: report download only; no API, or the API is a separate paid module; a raw feed is delivered without prioritization and without a choice of format.
16

How are pricing and licensing structured?

How it should be
  • A transparent model: scope and volume of work are agreed in advance, so the price is clear from the start.
  • No licenses for "assets" and no export limits (showcase services set, for example, a ceiling of 50,000 records); the volume of employee monitoring is agreed only to size the capacity.
  • No extra charges for modules, clicks or "talking to a consultant".
  • The client is an equal partner: the bet is on a long, mutually beneficial partnership with no hidden extras.
Red flag: no fixed price once the scope is agreed, hidden modules, licensing by "assets" and users, export limits.
17

Is there a pilot or a proof of concept on real data?

How it should be
  • A pilot on the client's own data, in the same report format as in production; the value is visible within 1 to 2 days.
  • An open demo: you can log in and look at the basic functionality right now, with no call with a manager, months of approvals or extra charges for modules.
Red flag: only a polished demo on someone else's data; to see anything at all you must fill in a pile of questionnaires, wait for approval and pay for modules, and the information about you and your infrastructure collected along the way goes who knows where and is used who knows how.

18

Where is your data processed, and does it pass through public AI and the cloud?

How it should be
  • No public AI: for many vendors "AI" means running your data through public AI chat services and cloud APIs, which is a leak into third-party channels; here the models are deployed on our own local hardware.
  • Only our own physical servers and no clouds at all: the database is never exposed outside; the principle is "any cloud is someone else's computer".
  • Isolation by client: each has their own portal that does not intersect with others, with a unique identifier, separate data encryption and an isolated server.
  • Only data that belongs to this specific client is used in the work.
Red flag: client data is run through public AI and the cloud; one shared environment for all clients without isolation or separate encryption; the vendor itself has had breaches or confirmed incidents, while a service that monitors breaches holds extremely sensitive data, and whoever could not protect themselves will not protect you.

Common questions

We already use a breach monitoring service. Why would we switch?

You don't have to take our word for it. With your permission we check your company and compare the result with what your current service shows. If we find accounts and risks it missed, there is a reason to talk. If we don't, you have had a free, independent check of your current vendor, with no obligation.

The employee already changed the password. Is the risk closed?

Often it is still open. People change a password by a character or two, or switch to an old one that has been in other breaches for years. If an infostealer is still on the machine, the new password leaks again, together with cookies and session tokens that get past two-factor authentication. That is why we check how passwords change across services and track what the stealer took along with the login.

We run penetration tests. Do we still need breach monitoring?

They cover different doors. Monitoring shows that employee passwords and accounts have surfaced in breaches. A penetration test finds a vulnerable subdomain with production keys. In one recent case the domain had no leaked email or password at all, so a monitoring feed would have shown nothing, yet public build files held 711 secrets, 120+ of them in one file. We cover both, because an attacker uses whichever way in works. Read the case.

How do you decide what to fix first?

Every finding gets a risk tier from a formula. Priority goes up when the account is corporate, when the record comes from an infostealer, and when a short or weak password matches the stealer trace. Each tier comes with what to fix now and what can wait, so the risk drops fastest. The same tiers are delivered through the API.

Is your dark web monitoring really the dark web?

Yes. Our team has long-standing access to real closed onion services and underground forums, where entry is by invitation or for a very high price. That is why we are among the first to learn about attacks being prepared, and why we get fresh data before it is repackaged and posted on semi-public forums. There, repackers post the same sets again to build up their rating, and the data fills with noise. How we deal with noisy records.

What do you keep besides logins and passwords?

The full context of each breach: cookies and sessions, API keys, tokens, SSH keys and certificates; documents, mail and database exports; full URLs with path and port, so an admin panel is told apart from a public page. The same account from different breaches is kept separately, so you see its history. The worst risk is often what sits next to the password.

Can you help find an insider?

Yes, together with you. You provide the context and the logs. We bring forensics, OSINT on the people involved and leak analytics: who created which passwords, when, how they changed them across services, and where the leaked data overlaps.

How large is your data set?

More than 106 billion records in our own database, plus another 46 billion raw records. We download and parse large volumes, including dumps that weigh terabytes, and our ETL pipeline also parses videos and PDF files, which many services skip because of the work involved. Raw data often has no password, yet it holds enough to build an almost complete digital profile of an employee for a targeted attack. Attacks on support desks and phishing are among the most common ways in, so such a profile is dangerous. Every source passes our validation before anything reaches you.

Do you use AI on our data?

Only local models on hardware we own. Public AI services are prohibited, and client data stays off cloud APIs.
