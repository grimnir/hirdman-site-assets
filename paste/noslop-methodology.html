The checks below help you judge the quality of breach monitoring, domain monitoring and brand protection, even without a specialist on staff.

Leaked data itself is easy to come by. AlienTXT-Base-562GB, a 562 GB torrent archive of leaks, is free for anyone to download, and a report that digests archives like it hands you data that is already in the open. What a monitoring service adds is analysis, risk assessment, prioritization and the time it saves your team.

Unverified data has a familiar cost. A CSV arrives full of repeated junk with fresh dates, as if the breach happened days ago. Your team and the SOC spend days on it, and the breach turns out to be several years old, or never to have existed at all. Asked where the data came from, the answer is "somewhere in the combo logs", and every finding becomes a new support ticket and days of waiting. An attacker uses the same data within hours. While your team digs through the junk, the real breach is lost in the noise, and attackers see it too. Sometimes one missed breach is enough to put the business at risk.

That is one red flag of many, so we collected the questions and the criteria for judging the answers. Ask to see each answer shown live on your own task, because anyone can write anything. Our own way of working, under each question, gives you a reference point.

Use these criteria to assess the service you already have; this page is useful on its own, whether or not you contact us. Clients often come to us to double-check the results of other contractors, which is why we show our methodology openly and stand behind its quality.

Data reliability and quality

01

Do you detect fake and generated breaches, and how exactly?

How we do it

  • We check through password recovery or reset whether the account really exists.
  • We weigh the reputation of the source and the forum: a known group with a history, or an actor with a negative rating who repackaged an old set as new.
  • We analyze patterns in the database to recognize generated passwords and synthetic records: identical salts, templated addresses, incremental IDs.
  • We cross-check against dumps already known, so a repack is identified as a repack before anyone takes it for a new breach.
  • We judge the quality of the primary source: a private forum and a first-hand dump weigh more than a worked-over set mixed with fakes.
  • We take the actor's motivation and the geopolitical context into account: during information conflicts, the number of breaches invented for publicity grows.

Red flags in an answer

  • The answer "we have filters", with the process left out.
  • A repackaged old set and a new breach treated as one and the same.
  • Generated records in the report, presented as genuine.
02

How do you determine the freshness of a breach and the real date of compromise?

How we do it

  • We judge freshness by where the data sits in its lifecycle; a date in the file says little. A breach has a path: intrusion, persistence in the infrastructure, the dump (sometimes with a ransom), posting on a semi-closed forum for reputation and tokens, spread across public channels, repackaging with junk mixed in, and release into public giveaways of ready login-password pairs ("free ULP").
  • We take data at the start of the chain. We monitor hundreds of hacker and ransomware groups directly in their channels, which gives us the dump right after it appears in semi-private circles, before it is polluted and repackaged.
  • We keep the date loaded into the database strictly apart from the date of the actual intrusion. As a rule only the attacker knows the real intrusion date, and until the database is worked through it cannot be established reliably, so every finding gets a confidence level instead of a false freshness. Even the attacker does not always know it: those who plant a web shell sometimes find someone was already inside, and intruders can stay in a company for years unnoticed. We therefore date a finding by when it appeared in the space we monitor, and confirm freshness by publication in the primary source and by the infrastructure inside the data: whether its domains, panels and software versions are live.
  • We have the infrastructure for the volume. Originals often weigh terabytes and are scattered across thousands of links; we collect them with parallel streams and parsers, and our processing pipeline extracts what is relevant and discards the junk. The useful part may sit in hundreds of thousands of PDFs, or in correspondence that holds a client's key.
  • We crack hashed passwords on powerful computing resources.
  • Age says little about a breach's danger. Human weaknesses persist: in our cases a ten-year-old password regularly fits a client's current service, so we check an old breach for relevance, whatever its date.

Red flags in an answer

  • Data taken at the end of the chain, from "free ULP" and public aggregators, already noisy and with planted fresh dates.
  • The date a record appeared in the database presented as the intrusion date.
  • Primary sources out of reach and too little infrastructure to process a large dump, so deeply buried items (a key in a PDF, a secret in correspondence) stay hidden and hashes stay uncracked.
  • A finding written off as old before anyone checks whether the password still works.
03

Do you work with infostealer logs, and if so, how?

How we do it

  • We work from raw dumps: they hold much that ready login-password pairs and combo lists lack.
  • We curate sources: we know who constantly generates and repackages junk, and we cut them off. We check combo lists with particular care.
  • We take raw infostealer logs when needed and cooperate with specialized teams that focus on logs.
  • After removing a stealer, companies often overlook shadow IT and the employee's personal devices, which leads to repeat compromise. We give specific recommendations for both.

Red flags in an answer

  • Combo lists taken from anywhere, with the source unchecked and junk generators still in the mix.
  • "Infostealer logs" that turn out to be the same login-password pairs, with the cookies, sessions and tokens missing.
  • A templated "change the password" after a stealer incident, leaving shadow IT and personal devices open.
04

Is your dark web a set of public onion sites, or real closed venues?

How we do it

  • We have real access from inside the environment: entry to closed forums is earned through reputation in the underground and through expensive paid accounts.
  • In this field the dark web means closed venues, the priority source of primary data.
  • Often this means downloading tens of terabytes and extracting only what is needed. Our ETL pipeline (Extract, Transform, Load) parses heterogeneous data from thousands of files into a single usable format.
  • This work is preventive: on closed venues we see attacks being prepared and planned and access being sold, so there is time to warn the client in advance, before there is anything to clean up.
  • The best indicator of source quality is our own product: we use the same data in our own penetration tests, so it meets the standard we set for ourselves.

Red flags in an answer

  • A public onion site presented as a closed source.
  • Access limited to what anyone can open, and too few resources to download and process large dumps.
  • Risks discovered after the fact, reactively.
05

Which sources do you monitor, and can you name a specific list?

How we do it

  • We monitor thousands of sources: public and private breach databases, dark web forums, data marketplaces, Telegram channels, ransomware group sites and infostealer logs. Our own database holds more than 106 billion records, plus another 46 billion raw records.
  • We cover group channels from two sides: through aggregators and public ransomware and breach trackers, and directly from the forums where hackers publish their personal channels on social networks and messengers (X, Facebook, Telegram, Matrix), plus our own accumulated lists. Public trackers are only a base layer on top of closed sources that few have access to.
  • We watch for early signals in news sites, leak aggregators and professional security and DLP channels, where a possible compromise is mentioned first.
  • We search on a trigger: when information about a potential breach appears or group activity spikes, including around geopolitical events, we deliberately search all available sources for the relevant databases.
  • Some sources are closed. We secure access to the private venues where fresh databases and logs are posted through our own methods of working with underground communities.
  • Partner sources in the underground deliver new breaches and logs among the first.
  • Every source goes through our verification: export, validation of authenticity, cleaning out fakes, normalization, and loading into the monitoring system through separate pipelines.

Red flags in an answer

  • A vague "many sources" in place of a specific list.
  • Collection limited to public Telegram channels and aggregators.
  • Coverage limited to breach databases.
  • Waiting for the database to surface on its own, with early signals and targeted search missing.

Depth of analysis

06

Do you account for 2FA/MFA bypass risks based on breaches?

How we do it

  • We take into account that a stealer log may hold a live session (cookies, tokens) that bypasses MFA. When such a log is available, we check with safe validation which services the session is still live for.
  • We assess the strength of the 2FA/MFA itself by an established method: which factor type is used, and whether it is vulnerable to session hijacking or a phishing kit.
  • With extensive penetration testing experience behind us, we collect 2FA/MFA bypass techniques systematically: the AiTM class (Evilginx, Tycoon) and fresh vulnerabilities (0-day, 1-day).
  • Most companies rely on MFA as their main defense, so all of this goes into extended recommendations: revoking active sessions, rotating tokens and secrets, invalidating cookies and re-authenticating, on top of a password change, on the assumption that the session may still be live.

Red flags in an answer

  • "Change the password" as the only advice, while the stealer may hold a live session that bypasses MFA.
  • The strength of the MFA itself left unassessed, and bypass techniques (AiTM, Evilginx, fresh vulnerabilities) left untracked.
07

Do you have a reverse check?

How we do it

  • We take a unique identifier from the breach (a login, the first part of an email, the password itself or a unique domain) and run it through all sources again and again, in different combinations.
  • Every iteration opens new links: one more service, a personal account with a password that fits the corporate one, predictable password-change patterns. This is how we reconstruct the attacker's whole access chain.
  • We account for the human factor: employees use one password at home and at work, so we check personal breaches along with corporate ones. The email shows the link even when the password differs: a personal account on a weak third-party site, a compromise, then work accounts.
  • We catch breaches with a bare "admin", "devsecops" or an ordinary Gmail address and no corporate email at all, and work out whether such a login belongs to a privileged account (administrator, moderator) or an ordinary user.
  • We built the method over many penetration tests: most attacks combine the human factor, password reuse and non-obvious links. The data has already leaked and the attacker may have used it, so the reverse check exposes these links and lowers the risk of repeat attacks by closing the routes the attacker could use to return.

Red flags in an answer

  • Superficial checks: "there is a login, a password, a URL, the password does not fit, risk closed".
  • Personal and corporate accounts treated as unrelated.
  • The email link lost once the password changes.
  • Privileges behind an anonymous login such as "admin" left unrecognized.

08

What scope of data do you process in breaches?

How we do it

  • We process login-password pairs together with raw dumps, files, correspondence, financial documents, backups and configs.
  • We keep cookies and sessions, keys, tokens and certificates.
  • We keep full URLs with ports, so we can tell the interface, the admin panel and the API apart.
  • The most critical risks often sit next to the password: internal correspondence, financial documents, customer personal data, backups, secrets and tokens. Attackers develop attacks with them even without a password.
  • A record with no password at all still has value. From a single phone number, social network profiles, correspondence and closed messenger groups are recovered; a dossier is assembled, and the attack develops without a single password, up to targeted phishing.
  • We store one account from different breaches separately, so the history of compromise and the links between incidents stay visible.

Red flags in an answer

  • Only the login, the password and the URL kept, with the rest aggressively normalized and scrubbed away.
  • An anonymized list of "such-and-such email was found", with the context missing.
09

Breaches as an investigation tool: do you take on such tasks?

How we do it

  • We determine the entry point (a service, a contractor, a device) and assess what else the attacker could have obtained from it.
  • Forensics: password analytics show who generated passwords, where and when, and how they changed them between services.
  • Insider search: the client provides the logs and the logic, then we bring in forensics, OSINT on employees and breach analysis, and search for intersections in the leaked data.
  • This is especially relevant now: layoffs are widespread, and hackers increasingly buy data and access from disgruntled former employees. An internal offender is more dangerous than an external one because they know how things work inside.
  • We have already closed such cases successfully. At a customer's request, we identified the internal offender behind a leak and resale of source code through a combination of data patterns, the reverse check and analysis of employee correspondence.
  • As part of brand protection, we check suspicious employees: what they write about the company, and where.

Red flags in an answer

  • A bare "fact of presence", with the cause and scale left out.
  • A list of breaches as the only output, with non-standard tasks (forensics, insider search) turned away.
10

Depth of coverage: basic breaches only, or reconnaissance through the attacker's eyes?

How we do it

  • At the reconnaissance stage we collect the same picture the attacker sees: subdomains and assets, open ports and services, resources behind VPN, vulnerable components and outdated versions (SCA), services without a password and insecure configurations.
  • We build a full inventory of digital assets: domains, subdomains, services, integrations, shadow IT. It shows the real attack surface, including what sits out of plain sight.
  • We search for secrets unconventionally, the way an attacker does: source code and JS bundles, forgotten configs and .env files, public repositories, and reverse engineering of APKs (keys, tokens and endpoints inside a build are a leak in themselves).
  • We find everything that grants access: API keys, CI/CD tokens, SSH keys, service accounts, tokens for AI tools. We check each key for whether it is live or expired and what it opens.
  • Along the way we check certificates, suspicious traffic (cloaking, redirects) and typosquatting domains.
  • This is a pre-pentest, lighter than a full penetration test: basic early checks with maximum return. On a domain with zero leaked emails, we found 711 secrets in public build files, 120+ of them in one .env file (GitLab and CI tokens, deploy keys, AWS roles).

Red flags in an answer

  • The database of leaked passwords as the whole scope.
  • Secrets in code, configs and mobile builds, and the external surface (ports, shadow IT, vulnerable services, certificates), left out of scope.
  • "No breaches on this domain" read as "all clear".

Usefulness, noise and delivery

11

How do you remove noise and confirm findings?

A false incident costs your team hours, and a missed real one costs the business.

How we do it

  • Only confirmed findings reach your portal; filtering out false positives is our job.
  • A word match is only a candidate. When a brand name coincides with an ordinary word or is used by other companies, keyword search produces a stream of junk, so we check every match for whether it belongs to your company specifically: by domain, infrastructure, employees and the context of the record.
  • We can show the share of confirmed findings.

Red flags in an answer

  • Weeks of your team's time spent tuning filters to sift out junk.
  • Brand search that amounts to a keyword grep, with other companies and random matches in the report.
12

What does the client get: an export with counters, or a report built for their goals?

How we do it

  • For each finding: what happened, what the risk is, what to fix first and how the attacker will proceed.
  • Priority comes from a formula-based model (tiers). It rises for a corporate account over a personal one, for a record from a stealer over one from an old public dump, and when a weak password matches a stealer trace. Each finding carries its evidence base.
  • We generate the report automatically from the analytics and the same risk model: ranked risk with recommendations.
  • On Enterprise, we write a detailed report built for your goals: identify an insider, find employees with poor digital hygiene or lower the overall risk. The work profile changes with the task.
  • At the customer's request, the report also covers employee risks.
  • We upload the finished report to the portal, where it is available for download at any time. It reads well for both the business and the SOC analyst: a technical breakdown plus an executive summary.

Red flags in an answer

  • A single line, "email found", with the context and priority missing.
  • An export with counters that you have to interpret yourself.
  • An interface overloaded with modules (radars, attack maps, news parsing) that leave the customer's risk where it was.
  • One report for everyone, whatever your goals.
13

Do you take the work on yourself, or does the client sort it out alone?

How we do it

  • You hand over only the domain; we run sources, reconnaissance, analysis and prioritization. Work starts within 1 to 2 days, without weeks of onboarding.
  • Search, deduplication, filtering of false positives, risk assessment and mitigation are on our side.
  • We keep the company under continuous watch, on our own initiative: new subdomains and assets, new open ports and services, infrastructure changes and, within the agreed scope, brand protection.
  • We track recurrence: whether the same thing has reappeared, or appeared on new infrastructure.
  • We alert you to what matters: a confirmed significant event.
  • Our reconnaissance uses the attacker's methods, and the result goes to you.

Red flags in an answer

  • "Here is your alert, the rest is on you" as the model.
  • A portal built for show that takes weeks to learn.
  • Infrastructure changes and new assets left untracked, so you learn about important things from the scheduled report.

Speed, integrations, price and trust

14

What is the delay between event and finding, and what does priority depend on?

How we do it

  • Part of the delay sits with the attacker: a hacker often posts a breach weeks or months after the intrusion. So we measure speed from the moment the data appears in the sources.
  • Once we detect a risk, we alert you immediately through a dedicated agreed channel, ahead of the scheduled report.
  • We track new domains and certificates in near real time (Certificate Transparency), and the ETL pipeline runs around the clock: a match with a client alerts our team at once, and a confirmed risk goes to the client immediately.
  • On Enterprise, we monitor VIP accounts hourly.
  • We process critical items, such as a secret in a public repository, out of turn, with forced priority.
  • The frequency of some tasks depends on the plan: compliance checks, for example, may run on a weekly cycle.

Red flags in an answer

  • A single batch cycle, "once a week or month", with everything at one priority.
  • Weeks of back-and-forth between the finding and the reaction.

Where is your data processed, and does it pass through public AI and the cloud?

How we do it

  • Our models run on our own local hardware. Running client data through public AI chat services and cloud APIs is a leak into third-party channels, so the data stays with us.
  • We use only our own physical servers, and the database stays inside them. Our principle: any cloud is someone else's computer.
  • We isolate clients: each has their own portal, separate from all others, with a unique identifier, separate data encryption and an isolated server.
  • We use only the data that belongs to that specific client.

Red flags in an answer

  • Client data run through public AI and the cloud.
  • One shared environment for all clients, under one encryption.
  • Breaches or confirmed incidents at the service itself. A breach monitoring service holds extremely sensitive data, and its own record shows how it will protect yours.

Common questions

We already use a breach monitoring service. Why would we switch?

Judge us by the result. With your permission we check your company, and you compare our findings with what your current service shows. Accounts and risks that only our check found are a reason to talk. If there are none, you have had a free, independent check of your current service, with no obligation.

The employee already changed the password. Is the risk closed?

Often it is still open. People change a password by a character or two, or switch to an old one that has been in other breaches for years. If an infostealer is still on the machine, the new password leaks again, together with cookies and session tokens that get past two-factor authentication. That is why we check how passwords change across services and track what the stealer took along with the login.

We run penetration tests. Do we still need breach monitoring?

They cover different doors. Monitoring shows that employee passwords and accounts have surfaced in breaches. A penetration test finds a vulnerable subdomain with production keys. In one recent case the domain had zero leaked emails or passwords, and public build files held 711 secrets, 120+ of them in one file; attack-surface reconnaissance is what found them. We cover both, because an attacker uses whichever way in works. Read the case.

How do you decide what to fix first?

Every finding gets a risk tier from a formula. Priority goes up when the account is corporate, when the record comes from an infostealer, and when a short or weak password matches the stealer trace. Each tier comes with what to fix now and what can wait, so the risk drops fastest. The same tiers are delivered through the API.

Is your dark web monitoring really the dark web?

Yes. Our team has long-standing access to real closed onion services and underground forums, where entry is by invitation or for a very high price. That is why we are among the first to learn about attacks being prepared, and why we get fresh data before it is repackaged and posted on semi-public forums. There, repackers post the same sets again to build up their rating, and the data fills with noise. How we deal with noisy records.

What do you keep besides logins and passwords?

The full context of each breach: cookies and sessions, API keys, tokens, SSH keys and certificates; documents, mail and database exports; full URLs with path and port, so an admin panel is told apart from a public page. The same account from different breaches is kept separately, so you see its history. The worst risk is often what sits next to the password.

Can you help find an insider?

Yes, together with you. You provide the context and the logs. We bring forensics, OSINT on the people involved and leak analytics: who created which passwords, when, how they changed them across services, and where the leaked data overlaps.

Why do you check our customers' accounts too?

Two patterns from our casework. In a leak, an ordinary customer login, even a bare nickname, can turn out to carry elevated rights. And attacks through the support desk, which rank third in our cases, start from a customer account: the attacker signs in as a customer and writes to your support team, and from a support network that is not isolated the attack moves on. We check customer accounts with the same care as staff accounts and report what to fix.

Why do you call your work a pre-pentest?

Because we start the way an attacker does: fast, cheap and through leaks. From your domain we map the group: companies that belong to it or once did, acquisitions, projects on the same source. We collect as many subdomains and their variations as we can, so each one ties back to you, and check that whole footprint against leaks. In the same information gathering we record open ports and services: a standard port may lead to your customer portal, a non-standard one on the same host to an admin panel that a leaked login opens. The infostealer logs show those ports and long, hidden admin paths, and we keep them. We also look at who works for you and their habits, as an attacker would, to find the weak spots. Each one becomes a note in the report with its fix. This is the work on every plan; at your request it grows into a full penetration test or a red team engagement.

How large is your data set?

More than 106 billion records in our own database, plus another 46 billion raw records. We download and parse large volumes, including dumps that weigh terabytes, and our ETL pipeline also parses videos and PDF files. Raw data often has no password, yet it holds enough to build an almost complete digital profile of an employee for a targeted attack. Attacks on support desks and phishing are among the most common ways in, so such a profile is dangerous. Every source passes our validation before anything reaches you.

Do you use AI on our data?

Only local models on hardware we own. Public AI services are prohibited, and client data stays off cloud APIs.
