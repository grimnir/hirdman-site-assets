One record is where the story starts.

A reverse check follows one breached record to every account it is linked to: the same person on other services, the same password pattern, the same mailbox name on a personal address or at a previous employer. It shows the path an attacker takes, before the attacker takes it.

After a password change the risk often stays open.

When a password leaks, the employee changes it, and the team closes the ticket and marks the risk as resolved. In practice the new password is usually one of three things.

The old password with two symbols added

The pattern stays, and if an infostealer is still on the workstation, the new password reaches the attacker the same way the old one did.

A favorite password from another service

A password the person has used for years on shops, forums and personal mail. It is already in breach datasets, and it is already in attackers' dictionaries.

A genuinely new password

This happens, and it is the exception. Attackers know these habits and build their dictionaries around them. In the first two cases the ticket says resolved while the risk remains, and a reverse check starts there.

What the rounds find

Five patterns behind most repeat breaches.

  1. 01

    The same email and password on a third-party service

    An employee signs up at a shop or a SaaS tool with the work email and the work password. When attackers breach the third party, the corporate credential is live in their hands.

  2. 02

    The same email with "different" passwords

    Summer2024!Mail and Summer2024!Shop are one pattern. Credential stuffing tries the family across dozens of services with one or two attempts each, below lockout and below monitoring.

  3. 03

    Different emails with the same unique password

    The password links a personal account and a corporate one. Breaking the weaker account is enough to get the password for the stronger one.

  4. 04

    A login without an email

    Records such as admin, support or test go through the same rounds. A unique password ties them to a company domain, and the login turns out to have administrative rights.

  5. 05

    The same mailbox name on other domains

    john.d@ at a personal provider and at a previous employer. A password from the old job opens an internal chat at the new one.

Questions about reverse checks.

Do you try the passwords you find?

Only with your written permission, and only against services you name. Until you give it, the check works on the data alone: links, patterns and sources.

How many rounds does a check run?

On Enterprise, until the picture is complete: the check runs across the whole company. Deep dive covers up to 10 accounts with up to 5 iterations. In practice one account yields 300 to 500 new data points.

What if the leaked record is ten years old?

We state the source date and the breach date separately, with a confidence level. An old record is still a starting point: the password pattern is usually alive.

Does this cover personal accounts of employees?

Yes, where they link to corporate access: a personal mailbox with the same password, a shop registered with the work email. We report the link and its fix; private content stays out of the report.
