IoT Applications in 2026: How Connected Devices Work and How to Build Them Safely

An IoT application connects physical devices to software: it collects what sensors measure, lets people and systems act on it, and sends commands back to the device. Since the early 2020s the number of devices has kept growing, connected products have become regulated, and botnets have grown with them. Below are the parts of an IoT system, where it is used, and the rules and threats that shape it in 2026.

12 November 2026, 5 min read

How many devices we are talking about

IoT Analytics expected 21.1 billion connected IoT devices by the end of 2025, 14% more than a year earlier, and forecasts about 39 billion by 2030. Ericsson's June 2026 Mobility Report puts cellular IoT connections at about 4.5 billion at the end of 2025, rising to 7.8 billion by 2031. The two sources count differently, and both show growth of around 10% a year or more.

What an IoT application is made of

  • Devices. Sensors, meters, cameras, wearables and machines, each with firmware that has to be updated securely for years.
  • Connectivity. Wi-Fi, Thread, Bluetooth Low Energy, LoRaWAN or cellular (LTE-M, NB-IoT, LTE Cat 1), chosen for range, power and cost.
  • Gateways and edge processing. Local computers that filter and analyze data next to the devices, so decisions do not wait for a round trip to the cloud and the system keeps working when the link drops.
  • The cloud backend. Device registry, message broker, data storage, rules and the APIs everything else calls.
  • Applications. Web dashboards and mobile apps for the people who use the system, plus integrations with business systems.

Each layer is a separate attack surface, and many compromises use the joins between them: a cloud API that trusts any device ID, a mobile app that holds a master key, a gateway with a default password.

Where IoT is used today

Smart homes

Thermostats, lights, locks and cameras are the IoT most people see at home. The Matter standard, maintained by the Connectivity Standards Alliance, lets devices from different brands work in the same home without a separate app for each. Matter 1.5 (November 2025) added cameras, blinds, gates and garage doors, soil sensors and energy tariff data, including bi-directional charging for electric vehicles.

Industry

Factories and utilities use sensors on motors, pumps and lines to spot wear before a failure and schedule maintenance around production. The data usually stays on site at first, at the edge, and only summaries reach the cloud. Factory control networks were built to run without interruption and were never meant to face the internet, so connecting them needs network segmentation and monitoring.

Healthcare

Remote patient monitoring sends readings from home devices to clinicians. In the United States, since 29 March 2023, section 524B of the Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act has applied to premarket submissions for cyber devices: medical devices that include software, can connect to the internet and could be vulnerable to cyber threats. The manufacturer must submit a plan for handling vulnerabilities after release, provide updates and patches, and supply a software bill of materials. The FDA's premarket cybersecurity guidance explains what it expects to see in a submission.

Agriculture

Soil moisture sensors, weather stations and connected irrigation let farmers water and fertilize based on what the field actually needs. Low-power networks such as LoRaWAN and NB-IoT carry small readings over long distances on battery power for years.

Cities

Street lighting that dims with traffic, sensors that report when bins are full, and air quality networks are common city projects. They tend to run for a decade or longer, which makes the plan for updates and replacement as important as the first deployment.

What changed for teams that build IoT

Security became a legal requirement

  • United Kingdom. Since 29 April 2024, consumer connectable products sold in the UK must not use universal default or easily guessed passwords, must publish how to report security issues, and must state the minimum period of security updates.
  • European Union. Cybersecurity requirements under the Radio Equipment Directive (Delegated Regulation 2022/30) have applied to internet-connected radio equipment since 1 August 2025. Since 11 September 2026, the Cyber Resilience Act has required manufacturers to report actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents, with an early warning within 24 hours. Most of its other obligations follow on 11 December 2027.
  • United States. The FCC's voluntary U.S. Cyber Trust Mark label for consumer IoT products is being set up, and the FCC asked for comment in October 2026 on the label design, certification renewal and post-market surveillance.

Botnets grew with the devices

Cheap connected devices with weak defaults still end up in botnets. In 2025 Cloudflare mitigated a record 31.4 Tbps DDoS attack that lasted 35 seconds. In the same report it estimates the Aisuru-Kimwolf botnet at 1 to 4 million infected hosts, mostly Android TV devices. In June 2025 the FBI warned about BadBox 2.0, a botnet of millions of streaming boxes, projectors, car infotainment units and digital picture frames, some infected before sale and others during setup through backdoored apps.

Older networks are switching off

Operators are switching off 3G, and 2G in many markets, to reuse the spectrum, as Ericsson notes. A device designed to last ten years needs a radio that will still have a network in ten years, which today points to LTE-M, NB-IoT or LTE Cat 1.

How to build an IoT product that lasts

  • Give every device a unique identity and credentials from the factory, and no shared default password.
  • Ship signed firmware with a tested over-the-air update path, and publish how long each product will receive updates.
  • Treat the cloud API as the main line of defense: check on the server that each device and user may touch only its own data.
  • Keep keys out of mobile apps and firmware images, which can be extracted.
  • Process data at the edge where latency or privacy calls for it, and send only what the backend needs.
  • Keep a software bill of materials and a public way to report vulnerabilities, which the CRA and FDA rules already expect.
  • Test the whole chain before launch: device, gateway, cloud API and apps.
