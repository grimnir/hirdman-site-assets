Penetration Testing in 2026: What It Finds and How to Run It Well

A penetration test answers a practical question: what could an attacker reach in your systems today, and what does it take to close that path? This guide explains what a test covers, how the main approaches differ, and what changed for testing programs in 2026.

12 November 2026, 6 min read

Why the question is sharper in 2026

For the first time in the 19 years of Verizon's Data Breach Investigations Report, exploiting a vulnerability was the most common way attackers got in. The 2026 edition attributes 31% of breaches to it, up from 20% a year earlier, while the use of stolen credentials as the entry point fell to 13%.

The supply of flaws keeps growing. More than 48,000 CVEs were published in 2025, a record by the count of CVE.ICU. No team patches all of them, so the useful question is which ones lead somewhere: to customer data, to a payment flow, to an administrator account.

Getting that wrong is expensive. IBM's 2026 Cost of a Data Breach Report puts the average breach at $4.99 million worldwide and $11.5 million in the United States, both record highs.

What a penetration test is

A penetration test is an authorized attack on systems you own, run by specialists inside a scope and time window you approve. Testers look for weaknesses, use them the way an attacker would, and document what they reached and how. The deliverable is a report with evidence for every finding and the steps to fix it.

A vulnerability scan compares versions and configurations against a database of known issues, and it is a useful part of the picture. A penetration test adds human judgement on top. Testers chain low-risk findings into a working attack and check business logic that no scanner understands, such as refunds, roles and approval flows. They also confirm which scanner alerts are real.

What gets tested

  • Web applications and APIs: access control between users and roles, injection, authentication and session handling, and the business logic behind each feature. Our API security testing guide covers this in detail.
  • Mobile applications: how the app stores data on the device, how it talks to its backend, and how much the backend trusts the app.
  • Networks and infrastructure: the external perimeter, internal segmentation, Active Directory, and the routes from an ordinary workstation to critical servers.
  • Cloud environments: identity and access policies, exposed storage, and the management interfaces of cloud services.
  • People and processes: phishing, phone pretexts and help desk procedures, described in our article on social engineering testing.

Black box, grey box and white box

The three approaches differ in how much the testers know before they start. More knowledge usually buys more coverage per day of testing. Less knowledge gives a closer imitation of an outside attacker.

Grey box is the usual starting point for applications. Attackers often begin with a valid login, bought from stealer logs or phished from an employee, and a grey box test starts from the same place.

How a test runs

  1. Scope and rules of engagement. Systems in scope, test accounts, time windows, contacts for urgent findings and anything off limits, written down and signed before testing begins.
  2. Reconnaissance. Mapping what is exposed: domains, services, application features, roles and API endpoints.
  3. Testing and exploitation. Testers work manually, following a methodology agreed in the scope (the OWASP Web Security Testing Guide for web, OWASP MASVS for mobile, PTES or NIST SP 800-115 for infrastructure), and use tools for coverage.
  4. Post-exploitation. Measuring how far a foothold leads: privilege escalation, movement to other systems, access to sensitive data.
  5. Reporting. Each finding with its severity, evidence, business impact and a fix the development team can apply. Critical issues go to your contact as soon as they are confirmed, ahead of the final report.
  6. Retest. After the fixes, testers repeat the attacks to confirm each issue is closed.

What changed for testing programs

Testing follows releases

Applications ship weekly or daily, so a test run once a year describes a version that has long been replaced. A common setup pairs a yearly full-scope test with short tests of new features before release and automated scanning in between. Continuous testing pays off when someone triages each automated finding and owns the fix.

AI on both sides

Attackers use language models to write convincing phishing, adapt exploits and probe applications faster. In IBM's 2026 study, one in four malicious breaches was AI-enabled, and those breaches cost $6 million on average. Testers use the same tools to speed up reconnaissance and repetitive checks, while business logic and the judgement about impact still depend on the tester.

AI features need their own tests

Chatbots and agents connected to internal data open new paths: prompt injection, data leaking through model output, and agents that call internal APIs with more privilege than the user has. The 2026 OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications lists these risks, and a test of an AI feature should cover them alongside the usual web checks.

The OWASP Top 10 was updated

The 2025 edition of the OWASP Top 10 keeps broken access control in first place, moves security misconfiguration up to second, and widens the old vulnerable components category into software supply chain failures at number three.

Regulators ask for evidence of testing

  • PCI DSS v4.0.1 requires penetration testing of the cardholder data environment at least once every 12 months and after significant changes, along with tests of network segmentation. Its future-dated requirements became mandatory on 31 March 2025 (PCI SSC).
  • DORA has applied to EU financial entities since January 2025. All in-scope entities test their ICT systems regularly, and entities designated by competent authorities also run threat-led penetration tests at least every three years (DORA Article 26), following the method set in Delegated Regulation (EU) 2025/1190.
  • The EU Cyber Resilience Act has required manufacturers of products with digital elements to report actively exploited vulnerabilities within 24 hours since 11 September 2026 (European Commission). A test before release finds those flaws before the 24-hour clock applies.
  • HIPAA: a January 2025 proposal from HHS would require covered entities and their business associates to run a penetration test at least once a year. It was still a proposal at the time of writing.

How to get a result you can act on

  • Test what changed. Point the test at new features, new integrations and anything that touches payments or personal data.
  • Give testers an account for every role. Access control bugs live between roles, and a login for each role is how testers find them.
  • Ask for the attack path. A list of findings tells you what to fix, and the path shows which single fix breaks the whole chain (our API article walks through an example).
  • Plan the retest when you plan the test. A finding counts as closed once a retest confirms it.
  • Write the report for two readers: an executive summary for management and auditors, and technical detail with reproduction steps for engineers.
